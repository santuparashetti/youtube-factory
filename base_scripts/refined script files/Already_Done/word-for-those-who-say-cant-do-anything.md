A bird once built a nest.

Inside the nest, it laid an egg—an eagle’s egg. It laid and hatched a chick. The chick hatched, pecked its way out, and emerged.

The mother of that chick was an eagle. Not just any eagle—its realm was not below, but high above in the open skies.

When it was time, the mother told the chick, "Look, I fly. You fly too."

The chick flew up a little, then came back down. It flew again, came back down, and managed to fly a bit more.

Then the little bird said, "How can I fly like you? I am small. Where is such strength in me? You fly so wonderfully; how can I ever do that?"

The eagle replied, "If you want to fly, try. Don't say it's impossible. That is what makes you unique. If your mother can fly, you can fly too. Put that in your head first. I fly, don't I? I am flying, and you are my child. Just try once; don't say you shouldn't try, don't say 'I have to live like this, but flying is impossible for me.'"

How does the eagle teach?

It simply watches. You feel within yourself, *one day I too should soar up high.* You just watch. "I will fly, I will descend, I will fly, I will descend"—you just keep watching.

One day it flew, then for two days it flew, and it kept flying like that. After eight days, it finally soared with absolute confidence!

It had forgotten that it couldn't fly; that doubt was completely gone. Even if it just flew a little, it flew like an eagle in the open sky!

At first, it feels like we don’t have it in us—"No, no, no," we think. But everything is possible. Can Indians not become mathematicians? Can Indians not become scientists? Can Indians not do brave things?

Bhagiratha brought the Ganga river down to earth, didn't he? Tell me. Originally, the Ganga did not flow here; it used to flow elsewhere. He went to the Himalayan peaks, blocked all the obstacles, mountains, and barriers, brought the river into India, and made it flow. To this day, Ganga flows. For thousands of years, Ganga has been flowing.

Water is water, food is food—what prosperity! That was Bhagiratha's effort. People call it "Bhagiratha’s effort." Everyone told Bhagiratha, "This is very difficult, very difficult." Bhagiratha replied, "That is why I am needed for this. It is difficult for you, not for me. Whatever is difficult, that is what a human being must do. I have come to this world to do just that."

What work did Bhagiratha do? How much value should we give him? After thousands of years of research, hard work, and effort, the whole of India became prosperous.

That is how you work. First, clear your mind: "What will happen to me?" Whatever your age may be, that doesn't matter. What if you grow old? Can't you stand up? Can't you walk? You should enjoy it—I can stand, I can walk, I can take two steps, I can climb two stairs. Enough!

As long as you are alive, stay vibrant. Don't sit with a sad face, looking as though God hasn't given you anything. "Why was I even born?"—both you and others might think that. But if you think like that, how can you live?

When you wake up, if your mother and father are smiling, the children will smile too. If they wear a gloomy face and wake up, the children will run out of the house. Don't think God hasn't given you anything. People with nothing have built empires!

Look at children who had nothing—no food in their stomachs—and they built empires!

Do you know "Jerome" (the watch company)? These watches are famous worldwide, a legendary brand across the globe. The boy who founded it was extremely poor. His life story is incredible. His mother couldn't feed him, so she called him and said, "Look, son, I cannot feed you anymore; it is becoming too difficult for me. I am half-starving myself just to live, but I want you to live fully. Go, wherever you go, build a good life for yourself, and become a great man."

Saying this, she gave him half a loaf of dry bread as her final gift. The boy didn't even have proper clothes on his body, nor shoes on his feet. With that half-loaf of dry bread, he walked out into the world. That was his background.

Yet, in his head, one thing was clear: "My mother said I must become great."

"I have to become very great. How great? So great that I can feed thousands of people—that is how great I want to be."

His mother sent him off. With no proper clothes, no food in his stomach, and just half a loaf of dry bread, he went out into the world.

He worked here and there, did odd jobs for two days at a carpenter's house, two days somewhere else, and his character was extraordinary. Working hard and progressing step by step, one day he built a watch.

He used copper and brass to build that watch. Then his wealth started flowing, and his business grew massively. Thousands of people started working under him. Every single day, he remembered one thing: "All this wealth belongs to my mother."

What deep emotion! What a feeling!

If he hadn't spoken those words—if he hadn't believed that he had the strength within—that power wouldn't have been expressed. A boy who couldn't even survive on his own now provides protection and livelihood to thousands of families. What an incredible saga!

Isn't that a legend?

How did he get that? By taking a simple flower, turning it into bread, and building an empire.

If you say "Yoga," it doesn't just mean physical exercise. I am weak—that is not yoga. I am strong—that is not yoga either. Everything is given by God; nothing is lacking. The question is how you use it. God has given everything—what hasn't He given? He gave hands—what hands! Hands that can build greatness, a mind that can think, a heart that can love. If you use your heart, you become a parent; if you use your mind, you become a scientist; if you use your hands, you become a karma yogi. Life is meant for becoming a yogi.

Why should age matter? Whether your hair turns white, whether you grow old, you shouldn't stop thinking. If you are alive, you must live fully!

The Vedas say:
*Pashyema sharadashatam* (May we see for a hundred autumns)

*Prabhravama sharadashatam* (May we speak for a hundred autumns)

*Shrunuyama sharadashatam* (May we hear for a hundred autumns)

*Jivema sharadashatam* (May we live for a hundred autumns)

*Adinaah syama sharadashatam* (May we live without depending on anyone for a hundred autumns)

*Shatam sharat* means a hundred years, a hundred autumn seasons. My eyes should see for a hundred autumns, my ears should hear for a hundred autumns, my feet should walk, my hands should work, and I must keep living. Not just living—*adinaah syama sharadashatam*—meaning I must live without depending on anyone. I should never be dependent on others; I must live with self-respect.

That is the language, that is the vision of these Indians.

*Sharad shatam*—not just any season, but the *Sharad* season. Do you know what the *Sharad* season is like? It is the most pleasant time. The rains have stopped around October and November, the earth is lush and green, water flows cleanly everywhere, the atmosphere is bright and clear, birds sing and dance, and the scenery is at its absolute best. To live for a hundred *Sharad* seasons means to live a life full of beauty, clarity, and joy.

We only know a few days of summer or a weekend off! We live for our weekends, take a few casual leaves, and call that a life. But true living means thriving through every season.

Even if you lie down on a bed of gold, if your spirit is broken, you cannot enjoy it. But if your spirit is alive, even lying on a simple bed, you feel like a king.

Vinoba Bhave—you must all know him. He was a great Mahatma, right-hand man of Mahatma Gandhi, an extraordinary saint, a lifelong brahmachari, who walked across the entire country spreading spirituality. He wrote the *Gitai* (a commentary on the Bhagavad Gita), which is read in homes across Maharashtra and beyond. He wrote profound spiritual texts.

Once, during the freedom movement, Vinoba Bhave was walking through villages. One day, his mother prepared food for him. You know that famous story. It was a day of celebration, and she had made *girda* chapatis (a special type of flatbread).

He looked at the food and said, "I cannot eat this."
His mother asked, "Why?"
He replied, "If we cannot even make a simple *girda* chapati, how can we fight the British Empire?"

If you cannot even make a simple chapati, how can you dream of changing the world?

Vinoba Bhave meant that if you cannot master the small things in life, you cannot achieve greatness. He wanted to finish the task at hand first.

No one should say, "I can't do it." You have to do it. You have to live fully, stand on your own feet, and build a meaningful life. That is the essence of true living.
