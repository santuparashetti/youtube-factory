### The Old Man's Wish

They tell a story from the olden days. There was a king. In that king's capital, there lived an old man who was about 70 years old.

He went to the Maharaja and said, "Maharaja, I have lived for 70 years, and I have lived well. I have a couple of children and a house. But still, tell me, what is there in life? It has just been a cycle of working and eating, but wealth never came."

He added, "I have one last wish, Maharaja. I want to die a rich man."

That was his mindset. He wanted to die being known as a wealthy man. There was no shortage of anything in his life—no shortage of food to eat or clothes to wear. There were people in his house and a roof over his head. But deep down, he harbored a sorrow that he had no hoarded wealth.

So, he went to the Maharaja and expressed his desire: "Maharaja, it is very difficult for me to live for many more days. Before I go, I want to die being known as a rich man."

### The Maharaja's Challenge

The Maharaja said, "Alright, I will grant it. I will give you as much as your heart desires, but on one condition."

"What is it?" asked the old man.

"A man must always earn his wealth. If I just give it to you, it will be like a beggar becoming rich. Do one job, and I will pay you according to your work," the Maharaja said.

"What is the job?" the old man asked.

The Maharaja explained, "There is a river near our capital. You have to go to the opposite bank and swim back to this bank. For every single round trip you complete, you will get one gold coin."

The old man was overjoyed. "Great sir, when should I start swimming?" he asked. In his 70 years of life, surrounded by such enthusiasm, he forgot his age. He forgot he was 70 and felt like he was 20. When the prospect of money arrives, 70 vanishes and turns into 20.

He went home and announced, "What absolute joy! Tomorrow, we will be rich."

### The Illusion of Wealth

The Maharaja had told him, "At sunrise, I will be there. The wealth will be ready. For every round trip you complete, you will receive not a 10-gram, but a beautiful 100-gram gold coin. Meaning, if you go 10 times, you get a kilo!"

The old man reasoned with himself: "I was a great swimmer in my youth. Even though I haven't swum in 50 years, watch how the mind works!"

The Maharaja had said, "Start in the morning. I, the ministers, and the townspeople will all gather there. You can swim."

The old man calculated: "From morning to evening, that's 12 hours. Is this a large river? It is a big river, but it has started looking small to me." When money is on the line and enthusiasm takes over, even massive obstacles begin to look small.

He thought, "What's the big deal about swimming? I can swim 10 round trips in an hour. Five minutes to go, five minutes to return—that's 10 minutes. That means one kilo per hour! In 12 hours, 12 kilos! So many lakhs for a kilo... 12 kilos!" He concluded, "Not a single person in this town will be as rich as me. The wealth hasn't even arrived, yet I am already rich."

### The Fatal Greed

Everyone gathered. The old man arrived. His children, wife, daughters-in-law, and all the townspeople came to watch him swim.

The Maharaja advised, "Swim carefully. Swim slowly. You have 12 hours. Swim only as much as you need to become rich." The Maharaja was very wise.

The Chief Minister grew anxious and whispered, "Maharaja, if you start giving away wealth like this, how will the treasury survive? Tomorrow another old man will come, then another, saying they want to swim too. What will happen then?"

The Maharaja simply replied, "Keep watching. See how the human mind works. Just sit quietly and observe."

The old man dove in and swam incredibly fast because he wanted to earn quickly. He went and came back. As soon as he returned, the Maharaja placed a coin and said, "Look at this, this is yours." Seeing the gold filled the old man with boundless energy.

He swam for an hour, then two hours. The coins started forming a pile. A mountain of wealth.

Afternoon came. He didn't drink water, nor did he eat food. Why? Because he had to earn! He completely forgot that we earn in order to eat. He abandoned food and focused entirely on earning. Every time he looked at the pile, it brought him immense joy. Going and coming, going and coming.

Evening fell. The wealth had formed a massive heap. "It's all mine," he thought. His children were equally thrilled, thinking, "We have become so rich!" The Chief Minister, on the other hand, was stressed about losing so much treasure.

The Maharaja, still watching, told the old man, "Isn't it enough? You have gathered plenty. That's enough."

"Maharaja, the sun hasn't set yet," the old man replied.
*See how he works!*
"It hasn't set yet. This is a golden opportunity. When Goddess Lakshmi comes to you on her own, would anyone reject her? There is still time, great sir. So what if my stomach is starving? So what if my body is tired?"

He continued swimming, but his pace slowed drastically. His body was severely exhausted. He had been swimming for 12 hours straight. Darkness was clouding his vision, but his eyes were still full of gold.

"I will go one last time, Maharaja," he said.
"Alright, it is your wish. Go," they replied.

The old man was utterly defeated; there was no strength left in his body. In his final attempt, he swam to the opposite bank and turned back. Just as he was about to touch the finishing bank—his heart failed.

The wealth laughed. The old man died.

### The Moral of the Story

The Maharaja laughed and said, "Do not worry, Chief Minister. Everyone's fate is like this. Our wealth will never truly belong to anyone; people will just leave it behind. That is what greed does to you from the inside."

Once greed takes root, it destroys us; it does not make us rich. No one has ever truly taken it with them; they only gather it and then leave. Everyone who has hoarded wealth has died, and only what they gathered remains.

Look around—kings and emperors have built magnificent palaces. What grandeur, what overflowing treasuries! For that wealth, they waged wars and did terrible things. And then they passed away.

This extreme greed, this toxic craving, ruins a person rather than bringing wealth or happiness. Therefore, the sage says, ***"Ma Gridhah"*** (Do not be greedy).

In Sanskrit, *Gridhah* means extreme craving. This relentless hunger stays, and it never fades. What was the use of all that abundance? The old man's corpse lay there. The wealth sat on one side, and his dead body lay on the other.

The Maharaja looked at the crowd and asked, "Who else wants to swim? You can swim tomorrow. All this is for you." Not a single person stepped forward; they all quietly left.

This is life.

*Ma Gridhah*. It is not just about wealth; anything in excess is dangerous. *Ma Gridhah*.

* Seeing shouldn't be excessive.
* Listening shouldn't be excessive.
* Working and laboring shouldn't be excessive.
* Eating shouldn't be excessive.

If we don't control excess, the country and the world will be destroyed. Hasn't it already started? A century ago, the global population was 1.5 billion; today it is 7 billion. India went from 300 million to 1.24 billion. This is excess.

If the mind constantly dictates, "I want more, I want more," it will lead to our ruin and the world's ruin.

That is why the sage advises, "Children, you must live, and live well. Life is what is important, not wealth. Accumulation should only be as much as is necessary."

We need just enough to survive and sustain life. We accumulate in order to live; life is not meant merely for accumulation.

What did the first type of person do? Earned, earned, earned, earned—and died.
What did the second type of person do? Earned, utilized it, earned, utilized it, enjoyed life—and then peacefully passed on.

These are the two paths. We must prioritize living. Wealth exists to enrich life, but life does not exist solely for wealth. It is not just us Indians who say this; the greatest economists in the world echo the exact same truth.
