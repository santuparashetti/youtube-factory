There was a forest. Inside the boundary of that forest, before entering it, there was an ashram. A yogi lived in that ashram.

Near the ashram, there was a major road passing through the forest. People from towns on this side of the forest had to take that road to reach towns on the other side, which was about 10 to 15 kilometers deep through the middle of the forest.

Once, a traveler arrived. By the time he reached the edge of the forest, the sun was setting. The sun was just dipping below the horizon.

He went to the yogi who lived there, offered his respects, and said, "Noble Sir, I need to reach that town along this path. How many kilometers is it?"

The yogi said, "Look, you cannot go now. It is getting dark. Stay here tonight and leave tomorrow morning."

But the traveler said, "That is my hometown on the other side. How is it possible to stay? I must reach home tonight." Having traveled from so far, his mind was intent on reaching home that very night.

The sage advised him, "It is a dense forest, and night is falling. No one is around now. Do not go. It will take hours, and it will get very late. At night, wild animals roam the forest. Do not go."

Yet he insisted, "No, Noble Sir, I must go. I must reach that town tonight." That town was his place of joy, which is why he was under such mental pressure and insisted on leaving.

Seeing his determination, the Guru said, "If you must go, I will give you a tool. Use it on your journey."

The Guru gave him a torch lamp (divatige) and a small container filled with enough oil. He said, "Look, there is oil in this container and here is the torch. Light it and go. The amount of oil here is just enough to cross this forest—if you hurry directly without looking here and there, the lamp will burn until you cross the forest. That is all the oil there is. Do not spill the oil, do not let the lamp go out, be careful. Do not put out the lamp, do not spill the oil, do not stop anywhere, and do not lose focus until you reach your destination."

This is the journey of life.

The traveler agreed, took both items, and set out into the forest. He held the torch in his hand and poured oil little by little as needed. The path was visible in the light. He covered half the distance.

By then, in the darkness and within the light of his torch, he noticed something shining near the path. He felt curious about what it was. He went closer to look and found a small pot.

When he uncovered its top slightly, he saw gold coins! Think about it—gold coins!

The lamp was burning, the oil was depleting, gold coins lay before him, and half the journey was still left. What a dilemma!

Wild animals were all around, but as long as the lamp burned, they stayed at a distance. In such a situation, this traveler stood there. He thought, "I have found such great wealth! I must take this home."

But how could he carry such a large pot at night? So he thought, "Let me count how much is in here, cover it, go home, and return tomorrow to pick it up."

He began counting the coins inside: one, two, three, four, five... As he counted, the oil kept burning down, the lamp kept burning, and time kept passing. He kept on counting.

He counted and counted. The human mind is very peculiar. As he counted, he found there were 999 gold coins—just one short of a thousand!

Look at how the mind works, what a trick! He thought, "I must have made a mistake in counting," and started counting all over again! "How could there be 999? There must be a full thousand! Whoever kept it must have kept a round number. Let me count again."

He counted and counted again. This time, the count came out to 1,001!

This is what is called *samsara* (worldly life)—an uneven, unpredictable life. It never feels complete. This is not just a story; life rarely feels like a perfect thousand. It is either one short or one extra. That is life: sometimes a little less, sometimes a little more, but rarely a perfect, complete state.

He counted a second time, then a third time. Once it was 999, once 1,001. Thus, he lost his sense of direction and time. Thinking, "Whatever happens, happens," he finally covered the pot, threw soil over it, and just as he did, the lamp went out!

It went out. While he was busy counting, the lamp burned out. The oil ran out, the lamp went out, and he was trapped in the middle of the forest surrounded by wild animals.

Think about it—surrounded by wild animals.

Then he remembered what the sage, the Guru, had warned: "Do not look here and there, walk straight on the path, do not pay attention to anything else. The oil is limited, the lamp will burn only for that long, just enough for you to complete your journey, not beyond that." He had been told all this! But what could be done? He lost his life while counting. The gold remained right there.

Counting was never finished, and the gold remained right there. He thought it was his, but he ended up in the jaws of a wild animal.

Wealth seems within reach, but it escapes us. It feels like we have obtained it—a person might feel they gained great wealth or a high position for 25 or 30 years, but slowly, bit by bit, it slips away from their hands. It feels as though we secured a high seat, but within five years, it is gone.

Life is very strange. Becoming a victim of useless attractions, life itself was lost. The wealth was found, but it was of no use. No use at all.

In the middle of the forest, he lost his life. It was as if the pot laughed at him and said, "You are not the only one; everyone who came along this path got consumed counting me!"

Many people! "I just sit here—sometimes 999, sometimes 1,001."

A little less or a little more—such a worldly life is like this pot. Sitting here, we keep counting and counting, and while we are busy counting, the flame of life gets extinguished.

*Anityani sharirani, vibhavo naiva Shashvataah* (Bodies are temporary, wealth is not eternal).

Whatever we earn, we ourselves will eventually pass away, or the wealth we earned will move away from us. One of these two is bound to happen; history is full of such instances. O human, do not assume this wealth will stay with you forever.

You are merely a traveler in this world. A noble Guru places a lamp in your hands—that lamp is the light of wisdom, the word. And he gives a little oil—that is focus and awareness.

With mindfulness and focus in this world, one must simply keep moving forward without letting the mind succumb to distractions.

*Anityani sharirani, vibhavo naiva Shashvataah*—no matter how much wealth we possess, that wealth will not remain ours permanently.

What is true wealth? Youth is wealth, life span is wealth, strength and capability are wealth, beauty is wealth, and external physical possessions are wealth. None of these stay permanently as ours.

Therefore, one should not live merely for these things; one should live for inner joy in this world.
